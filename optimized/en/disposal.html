How do you dispose of a lithium-ion battery?

Lithium-ion batteries and devices containing them must NOT go into general (household) waste or the recycling bin. They count as hazardous waste and, in transport, are classified as Class 9 dangerous goods.

Small household quantities belong at household waste recycling centres, battery collection points or in retail (producer take-back). Business quantities must be disposed of and documented through a registered waste carrier and permitted waste manager. To prevent fires, tape over the terminals and package damaged batteries individually.

Why must lithium-ion batteries not go into general waste?

Because they can be damaged and catch fire during transport or in sorting facilities. Lithium is highly reactive: short circuits, high temperatures or mechanical stress trigger an exothermic reaction that can hardly be stopped – so-called thermal propagation. This is exactly how the fires arise that have repeatedly hit recycling and sorting facilities in recent years.

There is also the raw-material aspect: lithium-ion batteries contain lithium, cobalt, nickel, manganese and graphite – materials regarded as critical raw materials. If they end up in general waste, they are lost to the cycle for good. Through orderly disposal, on the other hand, they can be recovered and reused.

Documentation of hazardous-waste movements

The movement of hazardous waste in the UK is documented under the Hazardous Waste (England and Wales) Regulations 2005:

  • Hazardous waste consignment note: every movement of hazardous battery waste must be accompanied by a consignment note (not a standard waste transfer note), giving origin, destination, EWC code and quantity.
  • Registered carrier and permitted sites: collection must be by a registered (upper-tier) waste carrier with the appropriate dangerous-goods authorisation; the receiving recycler must hold an Environment Agency environmental permit (and, for batteries, Approved Battery Treatment Operator status).
  • Record keeping: consignment notes and recycling evidence must be retained for at least three years. From October 2026 the new Digital Waste Tracking service replaces paper consignment notes with mandatory digital records.

Other relevant rules

  • Waste Batteries and Accumulators Regulations 2009: govern producer take-back, labelling and recycling obligations in the UK. (The EU Batteries Regulation 2023/1542 applies in Northern Ireland under the Windsor Framework, but not in Great Britain.)
  • Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment (WEEE) Regulations 2013: relevant for batteries removed from electrical and electronic equipment.
  • ADR / RID / IMDG / IATA-DGR: dangerous-goods law for transport – lithium batteries are Class 9 dangerous goods under UN 3480 or UN 3481. In Great Britain, ADR is applied to domestic carriage by the Carriage of Dangerous Goods and Use of Transportable Pressure Equipment Regulations 2009 (CDG 2009).
  • ADR special provision SP 376: determines, based on the battery's condition, the applicable packing instruction – from P903 for undamaged batteries to P911 / LP906 for critically defective batteries.

Batteries in a water bath and contaminated firewater

If a burning lithium battery is cooled with water or placed in a water bath, a second disposal problem arises: the firewater itself counts as hazardous waste. It contains fluorides and heavy metals such as cobalt, nickel and manganese – in concentrations that far exceed discharge limits for the sewer. Trade publications cite magnitudes of around 20 m³ of contaminated water per cooled vehicle.

Firewater must therefore neither soak away nor enter the sewer, but must be collected, stored on an interim basis and disposed of separately. We coordinate both from a single source: the battery itself and the contaminated water – so you don't have to manage two service providers in parallel.

Why is the disposal of lithium batteries so demanding?

There are essentially three reasons:

  1. Batteries are often firmly integrated into the electronics and can only be removed with difficulty without damage.
  2. During dismantling, cells can easily be damaged – professional separation requires special tools, procedures and checks.
  3. Through the exothermic reaction, damaged cells can swell and catch fire at high temperatures. High-capacity batteries are discharged beforehand to reduce this risk.

What you should avoid in battery disposal

You do not dispose of a lithium-ion battery with normal waste. Do not crush or puncture batteries and avoid anything that could bring the electrodes into contact and cause a short circuit.

Never package damaged and undamaged batteries together, tape over the terminals and do not store discarded batteries in large numbers or indoors – this considerably increases the fire risk. Suitable storage containers provide a remedy here.
